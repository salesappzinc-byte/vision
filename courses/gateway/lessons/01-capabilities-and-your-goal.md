# Lesson 1. What a chat tool can and cannot do

**Status:** Written draft, 7 November 2026. Not recorded. Not piloted. Not published.

**Objective:** Explain, in your own words, who a chat tool is for, why people use it, how it produces an answer, and what it is not, then name one goal that is yours.

**You finish with:** A plain-language card in the toolkit, written by you, not by the tool.

**Pace:** One sitting. Not tied to a calendar day.

## Spoken narration

Welcome. This lesson is about a tool you may have heard named and still not trust. We will keep the language plain. By the end you will be able to say what this kind of tool is good at, where it invents, and which one goal you want help with. You will write that down yourself.

Artificial intelligence, in this course, means a chat tool that writes a fresh reply from patterns in huge amounts of text. You type a message. People call that message a prompt. The tool continues in the shape of an answer. It is not a person. It does not open your files unless you paste them in. It does not know your customers, your family, or what happened this morning.

Who it is for: anyone with a language task they already understand well enough to check. A solo bookkeeper drafting a reminder. A creator trying a caption. A person planning a Saturday event. You do not need to code. Why people use it: a first draft is faster than a blank page, and a list of options is easier to edit than to invent from nothing. How it answers: it predicts words that usually follow words like yours. What it is not: a colleague, a calculator you can skip checking, or a machine that owes you income. A fluent paragraph can still be false.

Here is a fictional person we will reuse. Sam Okonkwo keeps books for a few neighbourhood shops. Sam is not real. The shops are called Shop A, Shop B, and Shop C so we never borrow a real name. Sam types: "Write a friendly reminder that March receipts are due Friday." The reply looks polished. It also names a shop Sam never mentioned, and it tells the reader to upload receipts to a portal that does not exist. That is a normal miss. The tool filled a gap with something that sounded likely. Sam deletes the extra shop and the portal, and keeps the polite order of the sentences. Sam is the person who decides what, if anything, gets sent. This course will not send it.

Your job in this lesson is smaller than Sam's email. Pick one goal for the whole course. Make it concrete and finishable. A work example: a clearer Monday note to yourself about which shop's books are waiting. A creator example: three honest captions for work you actually did. A personal example: a one-page plan for a library book swap, if you are not running a business. Write the goal in your own words before you ask a tool to improve it. If you let the tool choose your goal, you will not know whether the goal is yours.

If this website does not yet have a box to type into, use a chat tool you are allowed to use. If that tool costs money, the cost is yours and separate from this free course. You can also do the card with no tool at all. Do not paste passwords, identity numbers, bank details, or a real client's records. Sample names only.

Read your card aloud. If a friend would still ask "but what do you want?", add the missing piece. You stay in charge. A smooth answer is not a true one, and it is not a result you have earned.

## Presenter and demo steps

1. Show the lesson title and the objective on one slide. Say that the course is self-paced.
2. Define prompt, chat tool, and "you check the answer" with the four labels: who, why, how, what it is not.
3. On screen, type Sam's one-line prompt into a chat tool you have the right to use. Read the reply. Circle one invented detail. Delete it in a second typed line so the camera sees the edit. Do not send the email.
4. Point to the toolkit card. Fill one fictional goal for Sam, then tell learners to replace it with theirs.
5. Say clearly: no practice box is required, and any tool fee is not a course fee.

## Worked example (fictional)

**Label:** Fictional. Not a client, not a result, not a price.

Sam's card, before any tool rewrite:

> A chat tool drafts sentences from my prompt. It is for me, because I already know what a receipt reminder must include. I use it to get a first draft, not to know the truth. It guesses when I leave a gap. My goal for this course is a Monday note that lists which of Shop A, Shop B, and Shop C still owes me a folder of sample receipts. I will check every name before I keep a sentence.

Personal alternative, same shape:

> My goal is a one-page plan for a library book swap I am hosting for neighbours. The tool may list steps. I decide the date and I do not invent quotes from neighbours.

## Copy-ready prompt

Use this only after you have written a rough goal yourself. It asks for questions, not a finished life.

```
Ask me up to five questions, one at a time, to help me name one small goal for learning a chat tool.
Do not invent a business, a customer, a price, or a result.
Wait for my answer after each question.
When I say stop, restate my goal in two sentences using only details I gave you, and mark any gap with the words "you did not say".
```

## Your exercise

1. Watch or read the Sam example.
2. Without the tool, write five to eight lines: who the tool is for in your case, why you might use it, how an answer appears, what it is not, and one goal.
3. Optional: run the prompt above and compare. Keep your own sentences if the tool adds anything you did not say.
4. Save the card as the first page of your toolkit.

## Artifact

Plain-language card. Must be in your words. Sam's card is only a model.

## Self-check

- I can point to one place a tool might invent a fact.
- My goal names a thing I can finish, not "get better at AI".
- I did not paste private records.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a confident tone as proof.
- Letting the tool pick a goal you do not have.
- Copying Sam's shops into your card as if they were yours.
- Pasting a real customer list "just to try it".
