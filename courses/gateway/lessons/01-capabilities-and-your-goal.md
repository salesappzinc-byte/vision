# Lesson 1. What a chat tool can and cannot do

**Status:** Written draft, desk-edited 7 November 2026. Not recorded. Not piloted. Not published.

**Objective:** Explain, in your own words, who a chat tool is for, why people use it, how it produces an answer, and what it is not, then name one goal that is yours.

**You finish with:** A plain-language card in the toolkit, written by you, not by the tool.

**Pace:** One sitting. Not tied to a calendar day.

## Spoken narration

Welcome. This lesson is about a tool you may have heard named and still not trust. We will keep the language plain. By the end you will be able to say what this kind of tool is good at, where it invents, and which one goal you want help with. You will write that down yourself.

In this course, artificial intelligence means a chat page that writes a new reply from your message. That message is a prompt. The picture to keep is simple. The tool continues in the shape of an answer. It is not a person. It does not know your life, your family, or what happened this morning. Some tools can open files or send mail if you turn that on. Leave those controls off. This course never uses them.

Who it is for: a person who already understands the task well enough to check the words. Why people use it: a first draft is easier to edit than a blank page. How it answers: it produces words that fit the shape of a reply. That is a plain picture, not a full account of the machinery. What it is not: a colleague, a checker you can skip, or a machine that owes you income. A smooth paragraph can still be false. You decide what is kept.

Two fictional people return in every lesson. Sam Okonkwo writes notes about three labelled practice folders, Shop A, Shop B, and Shop C. Sam is not real. This is not a lesson in bookkeeping or tax. Sam does not tell anyone what they must file. Sam types: "Write a note to myself. The practice folder labelled Shop A is still on my desk." The reply looks finished. It also names a shop Sam never mentioned, and it says the papers are due on Friday. Sam never said that. Sam deletes the extra shop and the Friday line. Nothing is sent.

Nia Adeyemi, also fictional, is planning a neighbourhood book swap. No library has agreed. No date is set. Nia's goal is a one-page plan she can edit. The tool may list steps. Nia does not invent a quote from a neighbour.

Pick one goal you can finish. A work example, if you want Sam's shape: a Monday note that only lists which practice folder is still on the desk. A creator example: three honest captions for work you actually did, with no claim about views. A personal example: Nia's one-page plan. Write the goal yourself before you ask a tool to improve it.

This website does not provide the chat page. Open one you are allowed to use in your browser, or write the card with no tool. If the chat page costs money, that cost is yours and is not a course fee. Do not paste passwords, identity numbers, bank details, or anyone's real records.

Read the card, aloud or quietly. If it still does not say what you want, add that line. You stay in charge. A smooth answer is not a true one.

## Presenter and demo steps

1. Show the lesson title and the objective on one slide. Say that the course is self-paced.
2. Define prompt, chat tool, and "you check the answer" with the four labels: who, why, how, what it is not.
3. In an ordinary browser chat, type Sam's one-line prompt. Read the reply. Mark one invented detail in the text, or say it aloud. Delete it. Do not send anything.
4. Show the toolkit card. Read Sam's goal, then Nia's. Tell learners to replace both with their own.
5. Say clearly: no practice box is required, and any tool fee is not a course fee.

## Worked example (fictional)

**Label:** Fictional. Not a client, not a result, not a price.

Sam's card:

> A chat page drafts sentences from my prompt. It is for me when I am writing a note I can check. I use it to get a first draft, not to know the truth. It guesses when I leave a gap. My goal is a Monday note that lists which practice folder, Shop A, Shop B, or Shop C, is still on my desk. This is not advice about accounts or tax. I will check every name before I keep a sentence.

Nia's card, same shape, personal project:

> A chat page drafts sentences from my prompt. It is for me while I plan a book swap. I use it to list steps, not to decide the date. My goal is a one-page plan. No library has agreed. I will not invent a quote from a neighbour. I decide what stays.

## Copy-ready prompt

Use this only after you have written a rough goal yourself. It asks for questions, not a finished life.

```
Ask me up to five questions, one at a time, to help me name one small goal for learning a chat tool.
Do not invent a business, a customer, a price, or a result.
Wait for my answer after each question.
When I say stop, restate my goal in two sentences using only details I gave you, and mark any gap with the words "you did not say".
```

## Your exercise

1. Read Sam's card and Nia's card.
2. Without the tool, write five to eight lines: who the tool is for in your case, why you might use it, how an answer appears, what it is not, and one goal.
3. Optional: run the prompt above and compare. Keep your own sentences if the tool adds anything you did not say.
4. Save the card as the first page of your toolkit.

## Artifact

Plain-language card. Must be in your words. Sam's card is only a model.

## Self-check

- I can point to one place a tool might invent a fact.
- My goal names a thing I can finish, not "get better at AI".
- I did not paste private records.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a confident tone as proof.
- Letting the tool pick a goal you do not have.
- Copying Sam's shops into your card as if they were yours.
- Pasting a real customer list "just to try it".
